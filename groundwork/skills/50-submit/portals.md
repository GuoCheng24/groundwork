# Submission portals — the fields that are writing tasks in disguise

The form is not paperwork. Several of its fields are decisions that change who
reviews your paper, and several are documents you will otherwise write at
midnight.

## Do a dry run of the form, days early

Open the submission system and write down every field before you need them.
The ones that reliably surprise people:

- a **primary area / track** choice;
- a **significance or plain-language statement**;
- **suggested and excluded reviewers**;
- **conflict declarations**, which may require every co-author's affiliation
  history;
- an **AI-assistance declaration**, increasingly with a fixed list of categories
  to tick;
- **anonymous supplementary material**, as a separate archive with its own
  anonymity requirements.

## The track choice decides your reviewers

This is the highest-stakes field on the form and it looks administrative.

A paper making a **methodological** claim, submitted to an
applications-to-science track because its data come from a scientific
instrument, gets reviewed by people who will ask *where the scientific discovery
is* — and the honest answer is that there isn't one, because the contribution is
methodological. The same paper in the methods track is reviewed by people who
judge it on the claim it actually makes.

Decide the track from **what the paper claims**, not from where the data came
from. See [`../10-direction/venue-fit.md`](../10-direction/venue-fit.md), which
is the same question one level up.

## Eligibility and reciprocal-review rules

Large venues increasingly tie submission to reviewing obligations, with
exemptions for authors who do not qualify. Those rules have **specific, checkable
conditions** and a form field for each.

Check the condition against a public record rather than from memory — a
publication list, a database of past reviewing — and record what you checked.
"I think nobody on this paper qualifies" is not an answer to a field that asks
you to assert it.

## Anonymity is a property of the files, not of the text

Removing names from the title page is the easy part. What leaks:

- **document metadata** — author, company, machine name, editing-tool
  identifiers;
- **file paths** inside a supplementary archive;
- **self-citation phrasing** — "our previous work [7]" with [7] non-anonymised;
- **repository links** that resolve to a named account;
- **acknowledgements and funding**, which are commonly forgotten.

Scan the final archive, not the source directory. And remember the leak that is
easiest to miss: a note explaining what you anonymised, quoting the values you
removed, shipped inside the same archive. See
[`delivery.md`](delivery.md).

## The AI-assistance declaration

Where a venue provides a category list, tick what is true. Two practical notes:

- the categories are usually about **where** assistance was used — literature
  search, writing, coding, experiment design, proving — so tick per category
  rather than agonising over a single yes/no;
- a category you did **not** use should not be ticked defensively. An accurate
  declaration is the point.

This is separate from authorship metadata in a repository, which is a different
question with different conventions.

## After submission

- **note the revision window.** Many venues allow replacing the PDF up to a
  deadline after the abstract deadline; that window is when a late fix lands
  without a story;
- **the archived version and the submitted version drift.** Decide which one is
  authoritative and keep a tag for what was submitted, on what date;
- **record the identifiers**: submission number, forum id, archive id. They will
  be needed for the rebuttal and for the resubmission.

## Page and length limits, again

Limits are on the **built artifact** in the venue's own template, counted the
venue's way. Exemplars downloaded from an archive are author-formatted and
routinely over the limit. See
[`compliance.md`](compliance.md) and
[`../40-write/structure.md`](../40-write/structure.md).
