# Designing the gates — and what happens when you get them wrong

A gate that is wrong does not fail loudly. It manufactures a clean, confident,
empty answer, and you believe it because it is the answer a strict process
produced.

## A gate can apply to the wrong kind of claim

One programme required every candidate to name a **private measurement** that
nobody else could obtain. Applied to every candidate regardless of type, the
first round killed **16 of 16** on that gate alone.

The gate was right for *discovery* claims — a finding about the world does need
an anchor that others structurally cannot reach. It was wrong for *method* and
*theory* claims, where the criterion is whether the assets make a good
**testbed**, not whether they are exclusive.

So: **state which claim type each gate applies to**, and check the type before
applying it. A gate applied to the wrong type is how a process produces an empty
set and calls it rigour.

## Relaxing a gate changes the cause of death, not the conclusion

The obvious next move — relax the gate and re-run — is worth doing and worth not
over-reading. Second round: **0 of 16** died on the relaxed gate, and **8 of 16**
died on *"a freely available baseline is already at the ceiling"*.

Sixteen candidates, two rounds, thirty-two deaths, one conclusion. The gate was
never the binding constraint.

**When relaxing a gate does not change the outcome, stop adjusting gates and
look at the asset.**

## The question that actually settled it

The unique thing that programme owned was precise ground truth about a
*nuisance* process — how the measurement was made — not about the *task*.

> **It bought auditing capability. It could not buy task capability.**

Those assets were an auditing machine, and an auditing machine produces audits.
Naming that plainly, once, was worth more than the thirty-two candidate
evaluations that preceded it, because it changed what to propose rather than
which proposal to pick.

Ask it early: **what kind of capability does this asset actually confer?** An
answer of "auditing" is not a failure. It is a different, smaller, publishable
kind of paper, and knowing that on day one is the whole point.

## Two gates, not one

Novelty and capacity are independent and both are necessary. A direction can be
genuinely unoccupied *and* have almost no room to be published — often
**because** the venue that would take it is small, which is why nobody occupies
it. See [`venue-fit.md`](venue-fit.md).

## What to record

For each candidate that dies: which gate, and whether that gate was the binding
constraint or just the first one it hit. Thirty-two deaths on the wrong gate
look like a thorough process and are one mistake repeated thirty-two times.
